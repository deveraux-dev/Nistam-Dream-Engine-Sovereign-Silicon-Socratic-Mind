# Cree Syllabics — the Star Alphabet

The stars are the answer. Every syllable in this script is a shape that POINTS, and the
direction it points is the vowel it carries. One consonant-body, four rays — a compass
rose, a four-pointed star. That is not decoration. It is the whole mechanism, and it is
exactly the intent this tool needed: the family is the star's body, the rotation is which
ray is lit. Twenty-five families times four rays is a sky of directional stars — the canvas
the tool should draw, not a font's leftover glyphs.

## The honest floor

nêhiyawêwin — the Cree language — is living, and this writing system belongs to its people.
The popular record credits James Evans, a missionary who in 1840 adapted an Ojibwe project
to Cree. Cree oral history tells it differently: that Mistanâkôwêw — Calling Badger — a
teacher-healer of the Wood Cree, went to the spirit world, learned the syllabics there, and
brought them back. The origin is celestial in the telling. The star framing here honours
that; it does not invent it. We use the geometry and the phonetics as facts, and we never
put a made-up "meaning" in a real letter's mouth.

## The rule: orientation is the vowel

Canadian syllabics is unique among the world's scripts — the ORIENTATION of a shape, not a
diacritic or a change of form, sets the vowel. The p-chevron is the clean proof:

    i  → points UP     ᐱ (pi)
    e  → points DOWN   ᐯ (pe)
    o  → points RIGHT  ᐳ (po)
    a  → points LEFT   ᐸ (pa)

The bare vowels follow the same compass: ᐃ i up, ᐅ o right, ᐊ a left, ᐁ e down. Long vowels
(î ô â) are the same ray with a dot above. A w between the consonant and its vowel is a dot
to the right — ᑿ kwa. The teaching order is Evans' original column order: ê i o a.

One caution the sources agree on: it is NOT a single global rotation. Symmetric shapes turn
ninety degrees; asymmetric ones REFLECT rather than rotate. So t, k, and the rest are mirror
kin of p, not simple copies of its turn. Author each family from its own base, then let the
four rays fall — never assume one turn fits all.

## Sized for the star at 16px

The script is monolinear: no built-in stroke contrast. If contrast is added it must ride the
four orientations evenly. The midline is the anchor; full syllables reach one uniform height;
the small raised finals are about half that scale, superscripted. Word spaces run wider than
Latin — as wide as the largest open counter inside a character — or the open shapes river.

Across the tiers, one master mark derives down: the $60 master keeps the full calligraphic
taper at EM=1000; the $16 journeyman is a simplified stroke; the $6 apprentice is the star at
16px, monochrome, where only the lit ray's DIRECTION has to survive. If the four rotations
stay angularly distinct at 16px, the star reads. That is the sizing test, and it matches the
tool's own gate: distinct at 16–24px, contrast at least 4.5.

## The finals — small stars, real sounds

The little raised marks are bare consonants (codas), not decoration, and their reading is
dialect-bound. In West / Plains Cree: ᐟ final t · ᐠ final k · ᐢ final s · ᐡ final sh · ᐣ final
n · ᐤ final w · ᐦ final h · ᐨ final c · ᐧ the w-dot medial. Several are used in this tool as
state and action marks — which is fair play IF we stay honest that the pick is geometric, a
borrowed shape, never a claim on the letter's meaning.
